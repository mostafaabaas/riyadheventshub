Guide 1 · Planning

How to plan content coverage for a Riyadh event or festival

Successful event coverage is built well before the day, not on it. The content you see spread during a major festival is not a product of the moment — behind it is a written plan that decides what gets filmed, when it gets published, and who owns what. Here is how that plan is built:

Start from the event programme, not the camera

Turn the programme into a map of content moments: the opening, the key stage moments, the audience moments and their reactions, what happens backstage, and the closing. Assign every moment a format (short clip, photos, a quick interview) and a named owner. A moment nobody owns will not be filmed.

A written deliverables list per phase

  • Before the event: build-up and teaser content, guest arrivals and preparations, programme and line-up announcements.
  • During the event: short clips published in real time, selected photos, and quick interviews with guests and audience.
  • After the event: the day recap, the wrap-up film (aftermovie), and an organised photo and clip archive for later use.

Roles are clear, by name

A photographer, a videographer, a real-time editor and publisher, and someone following audience interaction. The role most often missing is the real-time publisher: excellent footage that stays on a memory card until the event ends builds no wave during the event.

Agree the approvals before the event

What needs approval before publishing, and what is approved once, in advance, to go out immediately? Real-time content dies in approval queues — a pre-agreed approval list is what keeps publishing alive while the event is running.

Think vertical first

Most event content is consumed on phones. Plan vertical framing and captions from the start, not as a later crop of horizontally shot material.

Guide 2 · Invitations & hosting

Inviting creators to Saudi events: permits, etiquette and practicalities

Inviting a creator to an event is not a "come and cover it" message — it is a small agreement with organisational, cultural and practical sides, and the more of it is written and clear from the start, the better the experience for both sides. This is general guidance for good organisation, not a substitute for each event's official requirements:

Permits and media accreditation: through the organiser, always

Filming and coverage accreditation inside any event runs through the organiser and the event's and venue's official channels, and it differs from one event and one venue to the next — so confirm it early, directly with the organiser, and never assume last season's arrangement applies to this season. Finalise your creator and crew lists after this point is confirmed, not before.

The written invitation: what it should contain

  • The date and the full schedule, the venue, and the access level granted to the creator and their team.
  • What the event hosts: travel, stay and transfers where they exist — or their absence, stated just as clearly.
  • What content is requested and what is optional: what is agreed to be posted, and what is left to the creator's judgement as goodwill from a good experience.
  • Embargo timings where they exist (material not to be published before a set moment), plus the official hashtag and the accounts to tag.

Respect the venue and the audience

Every venue has its rules on where filming is allowed, and attendees have their privacy — not everyone at an event consented to be filmed up close. Build the creator's programme around prayer times, the nature of the occasion and its expected dress. Briefing creators on all of this before they arrive is part of good hosting, not an afterthought.

Logistics for guests coming from out of town

Flights, stay and transfers arranged in advance, and one named host contact who answers the phone on the day. A creator who spends the event solving logistics will produce content about logistics, not about your event.

Posting: only what is agreed in writing is guaranteed

Attendance alone does not automatically mean posting. What you can count on is what the written invitation agreed — which is why the invitation deserves to be written well.

Guide 3 · Measurement

Measuring an event's content results: what to actually track

Measuring event content does not start after the event — it starts when you decide, beforehand, exactly what you will look at and where its numbers will come from. This is the practical tracking list we build our reports on. Notice there is no ready-made "typical" figure anywhere in it: every item is read from its real source in your event, not compared against an invented promise.

Inventory the content first

How many pieces were published, and by whom: the coverage team, the invited creators, the audience itself. This inventory is the base of every measurement after it — without it, the numbers have no context.

Reach and views — from the platforms' own analytics

Read reach and views piece by piece from each platform's analytics while they are current, and record them in the first days after the event, not weeks later. Rank the pieces from the top down to learn which moments and which formats actually carried the event.

Engagement quality, not just volume

Saves, shares and substantive comments say more than raw likes: a piece people save is a piece they intend to return to, and a piece they share is a piece they talk about your event with, in front of their own audience.

The audience's voice and content

Official hashtag and mention activity during the event compared with before it, and the count of pieces the audience made itself (UGC). This is the clearest sign of whether the participation idea you designed before the event worked.

The actions that follow viewing

Profile visits, link taps, and visits to the ticketing or registration page where your setup makes them trackable. Tracking links are prepared before the event — they cannot be invented after it.

Compare edition to edition

Define each metric once and reuse the same definition next edition. An archive measured consistently is what turns one event's report into the next event's plan.
