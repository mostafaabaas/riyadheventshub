Practical guides

Coverage planning

How do you plan content coverage for an event in Riyadh?

Start from the event programme itself: every stage segment, every booth opening and every crowd-peak moment is a "content moment" that needs a decision in advance — filmed or not, from which angle, published when. The plan is built on that map before anyone talks about cameras.

Split the work into three phases with separate deliverables lists: before the event (build-up content and speaker and guest announcements), during it (real-time publishing and a short daily film), and after it (wrap-up film, archive and report). Each phase has one named owner, and approvals are agreed in advance so real-time publishing never waits on emergency calls.

Shoot vertical first: your audience sees the event on a phone screen. Horizontal footage serves the wrap-up film and the archive, vertical serves stories and short clips — two crews or two cameras with clearly split duties, never one camera attempting both.

  • A content-moments map extracted from the official programme
  • A written deliverables list for each phase separately
  • Named roles: shooter, editor, real-time publisher, guest coordinator
  • Publishing approvals agreed before event day
Creator invitations

Inviting content creators to Saudi events: permits, etiquette and logistics

A methodological note: this guide is general practical guidance, not legal advice; media accreditation requirements differ from one event and venue to another and are confirmed with the organiser early. The golden rule: never assume a general invitation means a filming permit.

A good written invitation contains: the event's nature and timings, exactly what is expected of the guest (attendance only? coverage? how many posts?), what they receive in return, and the venue rules (restricted areas, attendee privacy, show filming limits). Every clause left vague today becomes a misunderstanding on event day.

For guests travelling in from other cities: arrange transport, accommodation and arrival times a week ahead, and share a short guide to the venue, parking and the reception point. Respecting a guest's time inside the event — a fast lane, a host who knows them by name, a reserved seat — is what separates an enthusiastic post from a polite one.

  • Media accreditation confirmed with the organiser before any invitation is sent
  • Posting agreements written and signed before the event
  • Venue rules and attendee privacy respected at all times
  • Out-of-town guest logistics arranged early
Measuring results

How do you measure an event's content results so the numbers survive questions?

Start with the inventory, not the numbers: collect every piece of content published about the event — from your accounts, from guests and from the audience — into one list with links. Any report that opens with a total figure and has no list behind it cannot be defended.

Take every figure from its own platform's analytics: views from the platform itself, engagement by type (saves and shares weigh more than likes), hashtag and mention activity, and the count of audience content pieces. Then tie results to trackable follow-on actions: ticket-page visits, next-edition registrations and sponsorship enquiries.

Keep the same definitions from edition to edition; the report's real value shows in comparison: this year's edition against the last under identical measures. And organise the archive by day and phase from closing day — an archive organised today is next edition's marketing material tomorrow.

  • A complete link inventory before any figure is quoted
  • Figures from platform analytics, never from estimation
  • Fixed definitions for edition-to-edition comparison
  • An organised archive delivered with the report

FAQ

Questions every organiser asks before a first event

Straight answers on the difference between coverage and marketing, timing, invitations, deliverables and coverage outside Riyadh.

What is the difference between covering an event and marketing it?

Coverage documents what happened: photos, video and interviews. Marketing starts before the event and extends after it: teaser content that builds anticipation, real-time publishing during the event that widens reach, and wrap-up content that turns a single day into assets you can use to sell the next edition and its sponsorships. We plan all three layers together in one deliverables list.

How early should work start before an event?

For large events we prefer to start four to six weeks out: two weeks to build the shot plan and guest lists, and two weeks for invitations, confirmations and teaser content. Smaller events can be prepared in ten days, but every early day adds options: better filming positions, stronger content guests, and calmer approvals.

Do you manage creator invitations end to end?

Yes: building a targeted list that fits the event's audience, drafting and sending the invitations, following up RSVPs, coordinating arrival and hosting on site, and tracking the posting agreed in writing after the event. Our working rule: anything not agreed in writing before the event is never expected after it.

What do we receive after the event ends?

Three things: a wrap-up film (aftermovie) that summarises the event, an organised content archive by day and phase holding every photo, clip and guest post, and a results report that documents every figure with its source from each platform's own analytics — no estimates. Final delivery lands within an agreed number of days after closing, and the first content batch goes out during the event itself.

Do you cover events outside Riyadh?

Yes — we cover Jeddah, Dammam and other Saudi cities, plus selected Gulf events. Filming and coordination teams are arranged locally in each city, and crew travel and accommodation are costed into the work plan from the start so there are no budget surprises.
